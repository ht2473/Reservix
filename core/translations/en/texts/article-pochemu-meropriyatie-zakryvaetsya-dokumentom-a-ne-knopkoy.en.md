In most development plan systems an activity is closed by changing its status:
the owner marks it as done and the percentage goes up. Such a plan is always one
hundred percent complete — because clicking a button costs nothing.

## What requiring evidence gives

Evidence can be a certificate, a report on a completed task, a work result or
a mentor's review. The requirement changes the meaning of the indicator: eighty
percent completion means that for eight items out of ten there is proof that
can be presented.

## What it costs

Some plans will remain open. The employee did the task but did not document the
result; the mentor held a review but did not write feedback. The "completed but
not confirmed" indicator exists precisely for this case and is shown
separately — it reflects the quality of documentation, not the quality of work.

## Where the requirement does not apply

For on-the-job development — rotation, project, stretch assignment — evidence
is not mandatory: for these the natural form of confirmation is the manager's
feedback, entered through the same mechanism, but enforcing it as a hard rule
would be mere formalism.

## Who checks

Evidence is checked by someone other than the person who attached it. The rule
is almost trivially simple, but without it verification degenerates into
self-confirmation.
