Filling a management position with an external candidate costs more than is
usually assumed. On top of the hiring cost comes the time to productivity: in
the practice of Russian companies, a middle manager hired from outside reaches
planned performance in six to nine months, while an internal candidate does so
in two to three. The difference is not in ability but in knowledge of the
context: links between units, unwritten approval rules, the history of past
decisions.

## When a talent pool is justified

A talent pool is not universal. It is justified where three conditions hold at
the same time:

* the position is critical — leaving it unfilled creates a risk to operations;
* a significant part of its requirements is specific to the organization;
* the time when it becomes vacant is at least roughly predictable.

If the third condition is not met, the talent pool degenerates into
preparation "just in case": people are listed as ready for years, lose
motivation and leave — often precisely because they were prepared.

## What a talent pool does not replace

A talent pool does not replace external hiring in two cases. The first is when
the organization needs a competency it does not have at all: there is nothing
to develop it from internally. The second is when a change of management
practice is required: an internal candidate reproduces the established way of
working that the replacement was meant to change.

> There is only one indicator that answers whether the talent pool works: the
> share of appointments to critical positions filled by talent pool members.
> The size of the pool on its own says nothing.
